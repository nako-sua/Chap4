## 4.3 Research Question 1: Current Adoption Levels

**Research Question 1:** *To what extent have digital technologies been adopted within the study context?*

Digital literacy, however, recorded the lowest mean (M = 3.29), suggesting a potential skills gap that may constrain more sophisticated digital engagement.

## 4.4 Research Question 2: Factors Influencing Adoption

**Research Question 2:** *What factors significantly influence digital technology adoption in the study context?*

### 4.4.1 Correlation Analysis

A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between the independent variables and the adoption index. Table 4.3 presents the results.

**Table 4.3: Pearson Correlation Matrix**

|                          | Adoption Index | Income Level | Education | Perceived Usefulness | Social Influence |
|--------------------------|:--------------:|:------------:|:---------:|:--------------------:|:----------------:|
| **Adoption Index**       | 1.000          |              |           |                      |                  |
| **Income Level**         | 0.512**        | 1.000        |           |                      |                  |
| **Education**            | 0.437**        | 0.398**      | 1.000     |                      |                  |
| **Perceived Usefulness** | 0.631**        | 0.281**      | 0.354**   | 1.000                |                  |
| **Social Influence**     | 0.489**        | 0.312**      | 0.276**   | 0.408**              | 1.000            |

*\*\* Correlation is significant at the 0.01 level (2-tailed)*

Perceived usefulness demonstrated the strongest positive correlation with digital technology adoption (r = 0.631, p < 0.01), indicating that individuals who perceive digital tools as beneficial are significantly more likely to adopt them. Income level (r = 0.512) and social influence (r = 0.489) were also strongly correlated, reinforcing the role of economic capacity and peer behaviour in shaping technology uptake.

### 4.4.2 Multiple Regression Analysis

To determine the combined predictive power of the independent variables, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted. Table 4.4 shows the regression coefficients and model summary.

**Table 4.4: Multiple Regression Analysis — Predictors of Digital Adoption**

| Predictor                | B      | Std. Error | β      | t-value | p-value |
|--------------------------|--------|------------|--------|---------|---------|
| (Constant)               | 0.821  | 0.214      | —      | 3.836   | < 0.001 |
| Perceived Usefulness     | 0.384  | 0.047      | 0.412  | 8.170   | < 0.001 |

The regression model was statistically significant, F(4, 315) = 127.38, p < 0.001, and explained **61.8% of the variance** in digital technology adoption (R² = 0.618). Perceived usefulness emerged as the most influential predictor (β = 0.412, p < 0.001), followed by income level (β = 0.289), social influence (β = 0.218), and education level (β = 0.143). These results are consistent with the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and lend empirical support to Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2 as stated in Chapter 1.

## 4.5 Research Question 3: Barriers to Adoption

**Research Question 3:** *What barriers do individuals and organisations face in adopting digital technologies?*

### 4.5.1 Thematic Analysis of Qualitative Data

Twenty in-depth interviews were conducted alongside the quantitative survey to capture lived experiences and contextual barriers not adequately captured by Likert-scale items. Using Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase thematic analysis framework, four overarching themes emerged from the data.

---

**Theme 1: Infrastructure Limitations**

The most pervasive barrier reported was the unreliability of telecommunications infrastructure. Fourteen of the twenty interviewees (70%) cited poor network coverage and frequent electricity disruptions as significant impediments to consistent digital engagement.

> *"Even when you have the phone and you know how to use it, if there is no light [electricity], or the network is bad, you cannot do anything. It frustrates you and you end up not bothering."*
> — Respondent 7, Male, 34, Self-employed

This finding corroborates studies by Agyemang et al. (2022) who documented the disproportionate effect of infrastructure gaps on digital inclusion in low- and middle-income contexts.

---

**Theme 2: Cost and Affordability**

Twelve respondents (60%) identified the cost of devices and data as prohibitive. While basic mobile phones have become relatively affordable, high-capacity smartphones and reliable data plans remain beyond the reach of lower-income segments.

> *"The phones that can do everything are expensive, and the data — if you finish your data, that's it. You stop. You cannot always top up."*
> — Respondent 14, Female, 27, Unemployed

These observations align with the regression findings in Section 4.4.2, where income level was identified as a key predictor of adoption, reinforcing the interplay between economic capacity and digital access.

---

**Theme 3: Digital Literacy Deficits**

Nine respondents (45%) expressed uncertainty or anxiety about using more advanced digital tools, particularly for financial transactions, official correspondence, and health-related platforms.

> *"I know how to send messages, but when they ask me to fill a form online or use the bank app, I'm not confident. What if I make a mistake and lose my money?"*
> — Respondent 3, Female, 51, Employed

This theme reinforces the relatively low mean score for digital literacy (M = 3.29) observed in the quantitative data and suggests that targeted skills development programmes are needed.

---

**Theme 4: Security Concerns and Trust Deficits**

Seven respondents (35%) expressed reluctance to engage with digital platforms due to concerns about fraud, data privacy, and identity theft.

> *"There are too many scammers now. They will call you pretending to be the bank or someone from government. I don't trust putting my information online."*
> — Respondent 19, Male, 44, Employed

Trust in digital systems is increasingly recognised as a critical enabler of adoption (Gefen et al., 2003). The prevalence of this concern suggests that regulatory frameworks and public awareness campaigns are needed to build confidence among prospective adopters.

## 4.6 Research Question 4: Impact of Digital Adoption on Outcomes

**Research Question 4:** *What is the relationship between digital technology adoption and socioeconomic outcomes?*

### 4.6.1 Comparative Analysis by Adoption Category

Table 4.5 compares reported household income change and business performance ratings between respondents classified as high adopters and low adopters.

**Table 4.5: Outcomes by Adoption Category**

| Outcome Measure                        | High Adopters (n = 190) | Low Adopters (n = 130) | t-value | p-value |
|----------------------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|---------|---------|
| Income growth (self-reported, %)       | 24.3% ± 11.2           | 9.7% ± 8.4             | 11.42   | < 0.001 |
| Business performance score (1–10)      | 7.4 ± 1.3              | 5.1 ± 1.7              | 12.87   | < 0.001 |
| Job access / employment satisfaction   | 6.9 ± 1.4              | 5.4 ± 1.6              | 8.61    | < 0.001 |
| Access to public services (score 1–10) | 7.1 ± 1.2              | 5.6 ± 1.5              | 9.23    | < 0.001 |

High adopters reported significantly better outcomes across all measures (all p < 0.001). The income growth differential — 24.3% versus 9.7% — is particularly striking and consistent with World Bank (2021) estimates attributing approximately 1–2 percentage points of GDP growth per decade to mobile internet penetration in developing economies.

### 4.6.2 Discussion

The strong, statistically significant associations between digital adoption and positive socioeconomic outcomes affirm the study's theoretical premise and the broader literature linking digital inclusion to development. However, a degree of caution is warranted: the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes causal inference. It is plausible that individuals with greater economic resources are simultaneously more likely to adopt technology *and* to report higher incomes, introducing potential reverse causality. Future longitudinal research is recommended to establish directionality.

Notwithstanding this limitation, the weight of evidence — across both quantitative and qualitative strands — points consistently to digital adoption as a meaningful driver of improved access to services, economic productivity, and employment satisfaction.

## 4.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter presented and analysed the study's empirical findings across four research questions. The key findings are summarised as follows:

1. **Adoption levels are generally high**, with an overall mean index of 3.65/5.00, driven particularly by mobile internet use (M = 4.12). However, digital literacy remains a notable weakness (M = 3.29).

2. **Perceived usefulness is the strongest predictor** of digital adoption (β = 0.412, p < 0.001), followed by income level, social influence, and education — together explaining 61.8% of variance in adoption behaviour.

3. **Four principal barriers** were identified qualitatively: infrastructure limitations, affordability constraints, digital literacy deficits, and trust and security concerns. These findings highlight the multidimensional nature of the digital divide.

4. **Digital adoption is significantly associated with positive outcomes**, including higher income growth, improved business performance, and greater access to public services. High adopters reported more than double the income growth rate of their low-adopting counterparts.

Chapter 5 will discuss these findings in relation to the broader theoretical and policy landscape, draw overarching conclusions, and present recommendations for practitioners, policymakers, and future researchers.
